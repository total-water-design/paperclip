## Pretreatment as an RO reliability system

Teach that SWRO pretreatment is not merely a turbidity-reduction step. It must manage multiple downstream failure modes.

Pearce organizes RO fouling conceptually into:

- adsorption of dissolved organic material;
- precipitation/inorganic deposits;
- particle deposition/cake or spacer blockage;
- biological growth.

Students should connect these to the existing Academy AOM/TEP, algal-bloom, scaling, concentration-polarization, and chlorination lessons.

### Common mistake

**“If SDI is low, the pretreatment problem is solved.”**

Correction: particle indices are useful but do not fully characterize dissolved-organic, biological, scaling, integrity, or seasonal risks.

---

## Conventional versus membrane pretreatment

Use Pearce to teach why MF/UF can provide a highly consistent particulate barrier and why this may improve RO reliability.

Potential benefits to investigate include:

- lower particle loading;
- more stable RO feed quality;
- lower cartridge consumption;
- lower RO cleaning frequency;
- potentially higher RO design flux/recovery where justified;
- reduced RO membrane replacement;
- smaller footprint in some cases;
- improved availability.

But the Academy must **not** conclude that membrane pretreatment is universally superior.

### Interactive mission — Conventional or UF/MF?

Students compare:

- stable offshore intake;
- variable coastal intake;
- bloom-prone site;
- constrained-footprint site.

Require a decision based on source variability, reliability, chemical use, waste, footprint, lifecycle economics, and downstream RO requirements.

---

## Membrane pretreatment does not eliminate RO biofouling

Teach that bacteria and biodegradable nutrients can still reach or develop within the RO system despite excellent particle removal.

This reinforces the distinction between:

- particulate barrier;
- membrane integrity;
- biological activity;
- assimilable nutrients;
- biofilm management.

### Common mistake

**“UF removes bacteria, therefore RO biofouling is eliminated.”**

Correction: barrier performance, integrity, nutrient availability, regrowth, upstream chemistry, and operational conditions all matter.

---

## Chlorination / oxidant position in this chapter

Pearce presents a skeptical view of chlorination upstream of RO in some SWRO contexts and discusses possible biodegradable breakdown products, downstream biofouling, membrane oxidation risk, and alternative biocide concepts.

Treat this exactly as the Academy treats the Khan chlorination paper: **informed engineering opinion / school of thought**, not a universal prohibition.

The learner-facing lesson should separate:

- intake chlorination;
- pretreatment chlorination;
- dechlorination before polyamide RO;
- membrane compatibility;
- chlorine demand/residual;
- DBP formation;
- AOM/TEP and biodegradable-organic effects;
- non-oxidizing biocide strategies;
- site-specific operator/vendor practice.

### Interactive debate — Chlorinate This Plant?

Students must defend a site-specific strategy rather than select a predetermined Academy answer.

Do not copy Pearce's historical temperature thresholds, dose examples, chemical names, or operating practices into universal design guidance.

## Coagulation before UF/MF for SWRO

The chapter includes historical trial experience showing that coagulation can improve or worsen UF performance depending on membrane material, water quality, dose, floc properties, and operating regime.

Teach:

- coagulation can improve cake properties or feed removal;
- too much ferric or weak floc can create carryover/iron fouling;
- membrane material and module architecture can change the response;
- variable or conditional dosing may be preferable to fixed dosing in some plants;
- source-water quality should drive chemical strategy.

### Critical safeguard

Do not teach Pearce's PES/PVDF comparisons as universal current polymer behavior, and do not hard-code historical CEB frequencies or coagulant practices.

### Interactive mission — Dose Only When Needed?

Students compare fixed-dose, variable-dose, and no-coagulant operating philosophies using permeability, ferric residual, fouling rate, filtrate quality, waste, and RO stability.

## Pretreatment economics and RO design coupling

Pearce's historical cost optimization demonstrates an important durable lesson:

> The energy optimum, CAPEX optimum, and Total Water Cost optimum do not necessarily occur at the same RO recovery or flux.

Teach the coupling among:

- pretreatment CAPEX;
- RO membrane area;
- design flux;
- recovery;
- osmotic pressure;
- high-pressure energy;
- cleaning frequency;
- membrane replacement;
- chemicals;
- cartridge filters;
- plant availability;
- amortization/cost of capital.

Do not use the 2011 SWC membrane specifications or Pacific/Mediterranean/Gulf case values as current design inputs.

### Interactive mission — Energy Optimum vs Water-Cost Optimum

Students compare several RO recoveries and pretreatment choices and identify why the minimum kWh/m³ may not equal the minimum lifecycle $/m³.

## Feed geography and design cases

Pearce compares Pacific, Mediterranean, and Gulf-type feeds to illustrate salinity/temperature/site effects.

Use these only as **scenario families**, not fixed regional presets.

Academy should teach that present-day design requires actual site-specific:

- salinity and ionic composition;
- temperature envelope;
- algae/AOM/TEP;
- turbidity/particles;
- bromide/organics;
- intake type/depth;
- seasonal events;
- current membrane product data.

---

## Environmental / sustainability comparison

Pearce includes a historical environmental-impact comparison between conventional and membrane pretreatment.

Preserve the more important lesson:

- sustainability depends on system boundaries and assumptions;
- concrete/footprint;
- embodied energy;
- power;
- chemical use;
- sludge/waste;
- transport;
- discharge impacts;
- replacement materials.

Treat any percentage environmental advantage reported in the book as study-specific, not universal.

### Interactive mission — The Greener Pretreatment Depends on the Boundary

Students evaluate two pretreatment trains under different electricity mixes, chemical supply chains, land constraints, and sludge-disposal routes.

## Guided Engineering Solution Mode

For difficult SWRO pretreatment cases use:

**source/intake → seasonal envelope → foulant mechanisms → pretreatment barriers → chemical strategy → UF/MF operating strategy → RO feed metrics → RO design/cleaning consequences → residuals → energy → lifecycle cost → final defense.**

Common-mistake cards should include:

- low SDI proves low biofouling risk;
- UF eliminates bacteria permanently;
- membrane pretreatment is always superior;
- more coagulant is safer;
- chlorination is always required / never acceptable;
- lowest-energy RO recovery equals lowest water cost;
- a 2011 regional case is a current design basis.
