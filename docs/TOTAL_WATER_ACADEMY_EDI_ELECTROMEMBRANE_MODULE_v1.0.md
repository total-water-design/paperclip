## Purpose

Add a rigorous but accessible learning thread on electrodialysis (ED), ion exchange (IX), electrodeionization (EDI/CEDI), and ultrapure-water polishing. The Academy should teach EDI as a hybrid electrochemical separation process that links membrane transport, ion exchange, electrical driving forces, mass transfer, water dissociation, pretreatment, controls, and integrated RO polishing.

The repository currently has no dedicated authoritative EDI project-design engine. Therefore Academy owns pedagogy and conceptual/educational exercises only. It must not invent a project-grade EDI sizing model. Numerical production design must wait for a validated TWDS owner or approved adapter.

## 1. Beginner physical picture — RO to EDI to ultrapure water

Teach the process first without equations:

- RO removes the bulk of dissolved salts and other contaminants.
- EDI is normally used as a polishing step for low-conductivity RO permeate when very high-purity water is required.
- EDI combines ion-exchange membranes, ion-exchange media/resins, and a direct-current electric field.
- Cations migrate toward the cathode and anions toward the anode through charge-selective membranes.
- Dilute/product compartments are depleted of ions while neighboring concentrate compartments collect transported ions.
- Ion-exchange resin provides a highly conductive path for ions in dilute water and supports electrochemical water splitting.
- H+ and OH− generated by water dissociation continuously regenerate the ion-exchange media in situ, avoiding conventional acid/caustic regeneration of the polishing resin.

Source basis: Rathi & Kumar (2020); Wood et al. (2010); Arar et al. (2014); Alvarado & Chen (2014).

## 2. Level 2 — electrically driven mass transfer

Map EDI into `L02-M05 Mass Transfer, Diffusion and Separation Fundamentals` so students see that transport can be driven by more than pressure or concentration alone.

Teach conceptually:

- diffusion from chemical/concentration gradients;
- electromigration from an electrical-potential gradient;
- convection from bulk fluid motion;
- electroneutrality;
- ion mobility and diffusion coefficient;
- Nernst–Einstein relationship as a bridge between diffusivity and electrical mobility;
- Nernst–Planck as a framework combining diffusion, migration and convection;
- transport numbers / current carried by ionic species;
- solid-phase and solution-phase ion transport through a resin-filled cell.

Advanced students may inspect equations and assumptions, but the objective is model literacy, not memorization.

Arar et al. explicitly frames ion flux through the ion exchanger as migration + diffusion + convection and relates mobility to diffusivity through Nernst–Einstein. Alvarado & Chen develops Nernst–Planck / porous-plug descriptions and links ion transport to resin-bed conductivity and electric-field distribution.

## 3. Level 4 — ion-exchange membranes, concentration polarization and EDI

Extend `L04-M01 Membrane Transport and Separation` with electrically driven ion-exchange membranes:

- fixed membrane charge;
- counterions versus co-ions;
- cation-exchange membrane (CEM) versus anion-exchange membrane (AEM);
- permselectivity;
- ion transport through membrane and resin phases;
- dilute and concentrate compartments;
- mixed-bed versus layered-bed resin concepts;
- plate-and-frame versus spiral-wound CEDI construction as device-literacy examples;
- thin-cell versus thick-cell concepts as historical/device-design literacy.

Wood et al. describes the two principal commercial CEDI arrangements as plate-and-frame and spiral-wound and distinguishes thin- and thick-cell construction. These dimensions and historical market-share observations are source-specific examples, not universal 2026 design standards.

### Concentration polarization and limiting current

Connect to `L04-M03 Concentration Polarization, Activity, Scaling & Fouling`.

Students should understand the sequence:

bulk ion transport -> depletion near an ion-selective membrane -> concentration polarization -> increased resistance -> limiting-current behavior -> water dissociation / changed pH behavior.

Important nuance:

- In conventional ED, limiting-current operation is commonly treated as an efficiency / polarization boundary and operation may be selected below that boundary.
- In CEDI, controlled water dissociation and the resulting H+/OH− electroregeneration are integral to deep deionization and resin regeneration.
- Therefore a simple ED rule such as “always operate at 80% of limiting current” must **not** be promoted as a universal EDI rule.

The Academy must teach why the role of water splitting differs between ED and EDI rather than collapsing both technologies into one operating rule.

## 4. Enhanced-transfer and electroregeneration regimes

Wood et al. identifies two useful conceptual regimes for CEDI:

1. **Enhanced transfer** — resin remains largely in salt form and provides an ion-conducting path through otherwise low-conductivity water.
2. **Electroregeneration** — electrically induced water dissociation continuously creates H+ and OH−, regenerating resin to H/OH forms and enabling deep removal of weakly ionized species.

Use this as the organizing physical explanation for why resin is present inside an EDI diluting compartment.

## 5. Weakly ionized species: CO2 / carbonic acid, silica and boron

Use EDI as an advanced lesson in acid-base chemistry and speciation.

Students should reason that weakly ionized species can be difficult for purely ion-selective separations when the species is neutral or only partly ionized. The electroregeneration environment can support deeper removal of weak acids once local H+/OH− chemistry and ion exchange are considered.

Wood et al. discusses carbonic and silicic acid removal in the electroregeneration regime. Arar et al. reviews published EDI work involving boron and silica from RO permeate.

Do not turn the numerical removal percentages from individual laboratory studies into universal product guarantees. Use them only as source-labelled experimental examples.

## 6. Pretreatment and RO→EDI integration

Teach that EDI is a polishing technology with stricter feed-water quality requirements than a conventional regenerable demineralizer in many applications.

Wood et al. emphasizes that EDI lacks the periodic acid/caustic cleaning effect inherent in chemically regenerated IX, making pretreatment particularly important; RO is normally installed upstream in ultrapure-water service.

Students should check qualitatively for:

- feed conductivity / TDS;
- hardness and precipitation risk;
- silica;
- dissolved CO2 / alkalinity system;
- organics / TOC;
- Fe/Mn and other foulants;
- oxidant compatibility;
- temperature;
- pressure;
- concentrate flow sufficient to avoid excessive scaling risk;
- membrane/resin compatibility.

Historical “typical feed requirements” reported by Wood et al. (2010) combine multiple manufacturer specifications and must be taught only as a historical/example envelope. Current project design must use the current selected manufacturer’s limits and validated TWDS logic.

## 7. Hydrodynamics, residence time and flow

Arar et al. highlights flow velocity as a major performance variable and illustrates the competing effects of hydrodynamics and residence time:

- increasing flow can reduce boundary-layer thickness and improve mass transfer;
- increasing flow also reduces residence time;
- therefore “more flow is always better” is false.

This is an excellent bridge back to Unit Operations and a candidate interactive exercise.

## 8. Electrical design and energy connection — Level 6

Map EDI into `L06-M04 Soft Starters, VFDs and Process Energy` as an example of a process where the **electrical field is itself the separation driving force**.

Teach:

- DC power supply concept;
- voltage, current and current density;
- electrical power = V × I at a system level;
- energy per treated-water volume;
- cell/stack electrical resistance;
- why lower feed conductivity changes electrical behavior;
- current efficiency;
- limiting current and overlimiting/water-splitting behavior;
- electrical energy versus chemical-regeneration tradeoff.

Numerical energy values in the source papers are examples tied to specific devices and operating conditions, not universal TWDS guarantees.

## 9. Instrumentation, controls and protection — Level 7

Use EDI as a practical controls case study in `L07-M01` and `L07-M04`.

Candidate monitored variables:

- feed/product conductivity or resistivity;
- dilute flow;
- concentrate flow;
- electrode-rinse flow where applicable;
- inlet/outlet pressure and differential pressure;
- temperature;
- DC voltage;
- DC current/current density;
- product-quality divert criteria;
- concentrate conductivity;
- leak / low-flow / high-pressure / high-temperature / power-supply alarms.

Candidate permissives/interlocks:

- do not energize the stack without required flows;
- protect against low concentrate flow that could increase scaling risk;
- shut down or divert if product resistivity/conductivity is outside specification;
- manage startup/flush before applying full current;
- protect the power supply and stack from abnormal pressure, temperature or electrical conditions.

These are educational control concepts. Exact trip values belong to the selected OEM / validated design owner.

## 10. Technology selection — Level 9

Use `L09-M04 Integrated Treatment-Train Comparison` to compare:

- RO + regenerable mixed-bed IX;
- RO + CEDI;
- ED/EDR where appropriate;
- RO/EDI combinations for high-purity water.

Students should compare:

- product-water quality;
- chemical-regeneration burden;
- wastewater/regenerant waste;
- electrical consumption;
- pretreatment stringency;
- scaling/fouling sensitivity;
- footprint;
- continuity of operation;
- maintenance philosophy;
- feed-water variability;
- vendor operating envelope;
- economics and lifecycle risk.

Wood et al. reports the commercial advantages of continuous operation and eliminating acid/caustic regeneration, while also stressing the greater importance of feed pretreatment. Those tradeoffs are ideal for an engineering decision exercise.

## 12. Interactive Academy missions

1. **Build the CEDI cell** — place AEM/CEM, resin, dilute, concentrate and electrodes correctly.
2. **Follow the ion** — trace Na+, Ca2+, Cl− and SO4²− under an electric field and membrane selectivity.
3. **Transport-mechanism mixer** — identify diffusion, electromigration and convection contributions.
4. **Current–voltage explorer** — identify ohmic/transport-limited behavior and explain limiting current conceptually.
5. **ED versus EDI water-splitting challenge** — explain why water splitting is generally undesirable as an ED efficiency penalty yet intentionally used for EDI electroregeneration.
6. **Flow-rate tradeoff mission** — balance boundary-layer mass transfer against residence time.
7. **RO→EDI feed-quality gate** — decide whether a proposed RO permeate is appropriate for an EDI OEM review.
8. **Silica/boron chemistry mission** — connect weak-acid speciation to polishing difficulty without using an Academy-owned design equation.
9. **EDI controls mission** — place conductivity/resistivity, pressure, temperature, flow and electrical measurements and define permissives.
10. **Polishing technology decision** — compare regenerable IX versus CEDI for a high-purity-water project.
11. **Historical limit detective** — distinguish a 2010 literature/example feed specification from a current manufacturer design limit.
12. **Vendor-data handoff** — identify the missing OEM inputs required before a real EDI module-selection calculation can be performed.

## 14. Scientific-age and authority policy

The source set spans 2010–2020 and is valuable for fundamentals, mechanisms and historical/device context. However:

- current OEM module capacities, feed limits, pressure limits, silica/hardness limits, current densities, voltage ranges and energy guarantees require current manufacturer documentation;
- individual experimental removal efficiencies must remain source-labelled examples;
- historical claims about installed market share or module construction should not be presented as current 2026 market facts without verification;
- material labelled “current state of the art” requires a newer literature/OEM review.
