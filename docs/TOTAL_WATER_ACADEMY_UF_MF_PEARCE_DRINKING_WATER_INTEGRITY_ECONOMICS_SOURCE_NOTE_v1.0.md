# 2. Dissolved organic carbon, NOM and AOC

Pearce distinguishes multiple organic-water-quality concepts that students often collapse into a single term.

Teach:

- **DOC** as dissolved organic carbon measured operationally after a defined filtration step;
- **NOM** as a broad family of naturally occurring organic matter;
- **TOC** as total organic carbon;
- **AOC** as assimilable organic carbon, a biologically relevant subset associated with microbial growth potential;
- biodegradability and molecular character matter as well as bulk carbon concentration;
- a treatment step can change the character of organic matter even when total carbon removal is modest.

### Common mistake

**“If UF removes turbidity and SDI is low, organic/biofouling risk is solved.”**

Why it fails:

UF/MF is primarily a particulate/colloidal barrier. Dissolved biodegradable organic matter may remain and can still influence downstream biological stability or membrane biofouling.

### Interactive exercise — Carbon Is Not One Number

Give learners TOC, DOC, AOC and biopolymer data from two waters and ask which water presents the greater downstream biological-growth risk and what additional evidence is needed.

---

# 3. Disinfection and organic-matter interactions

Use the source to reinforce that disinfection cannot be taught separately from source-water chemistry.

Teach conceptually:

- disinfectant demand consumes oxidant;
- dissolved organics can form disinfection by-products under certain disinfectants;
- upstream precursor removal can influence downstream disinfection chemistry;
- ozone, chlorine dioxide, chlorine and chloramine have different chemistry and operating tradeoffs;
- membrane treatment can reduce particulate microbial load but does not eliminate the need to understand finished-water disinfection requirements.

Do not treat Pearce's 2011 statements as current jurisdictional disinfection standards.

### Common mistake

**“Membrane filtration replaces disinfection.”**

Correction:

A membrane barrier and a disinfection barrier solve different risks and are governed by different integrity/contact/residual requirements.

---

# 4. Iron and manganese: oxidation before membrane filtration

The pages provide a good bridge between water chemistry and membrane pretreatment.

Teach:

1. dissolved Fe/Mn may exist in soluble reduced form;
2. oxidation can convert soluble species to insoluble oxides/hydroxides;
3. precipitation kinetics and resulting particle size affect filtration;
4. pH, oxidant type, temperature and catalytic surfaces can influence oxidation rate;
5. the membrane should not be asked to solve a chemistry problem that was not properly conditioned upstream;
6. very fine precipitates can be more difficult to filter than properly aged/formed solids.

### Common mistake

**“If Fe or Mn can precipitate, just let it precipitate directly on the UF.”**

Why it fails:

Uncontrolled precipitation can create fine, sticky or poorly filterable solids and can increase irreversible fouling.

### Exercise — Oxidize First or Filter First?

Student receives dissolved Fe/Mn, pH, oxidant options, contact time and membrane configuration and must define what evidence is required before choosing the pretreatment sequence.

---

# 5. Precipitation kinetics matter

The source explicitly discusses the fact that precipitation/oxidation is not instantaneous.

Teach:

- thermodynamic feasibility does not prove fast reaction;
- nucleation and growth may require time;
- catalyst surfaces can accelerate oxidation;
- temperature can influence kinetics;
- adequate contact time and mixing can matter;
- the desired solid form and size matter to downstream filtration.

Connect this to the Academy's existing crystallization/nucleation thread.

### Common mistake

**“The saturation or redox calculation says it should precipitate, therefore it is already a filterable particle.”**

Correction:

Equilibrium direction and reaction kinetics are different engineering questions.

# 6. Microbiological contaminants

Teach microbiological barrier thinking without making current regulatory-credit claims from the 2011 source.

## 6.1 Viruses and bacteria

Teach:

- bacteria and viruses differ greatly in size;
- removal mechanisms depend on membrane pore distribution, adsorption, integrity and system operation;
- nominal pore size alone is not a validated log-removal claim;
- surrogate organisms may be used in controlled challenge testing;
- a physically intact membrane can provide strong particle/microorganism removal, but actual credit depends on validated evidence and current regulation.

The source discusses MS2 bacteriophage as an historical surrogate/example for virus challenge testing.

### Common mistake

**“Pore size smaller than the organism means guaranteed LRV.”**

Correction:

Validated removal must consider membrane defects, pore-size distribution, challenge organism, test method, concentration, sampling and regulatory acceptance.

## 6.2 Protozoa / Cryptosporidium and Giardia

Teach:

- protozoan cysts/oocysts are much larger than viruses;
- they can be resistant to some conventional disinfectants;
- membrane filtration can be an effective physical barrier when integrity is maintained;
- barrier credit must be supported by accepted challenge/integrity evidence.

### Interactive exercise — Build the Microbial Barrier

Student chooses the role of coagulation, UF/MF, UV/disinfection and distribution residual for a surface-water plant, identifying which risk each step addresses.

# 7. Challenge testing

Pearce provides an example challenge-test workflow.

Academy should teach the structure, not duplicate the exact 2011 protocol as a current standard.

Teach the generic logic:

1. define target organism/surrogate;
2. establish challenge concentration;
3. establish baseline/inlet concentration;
4. collect permeate samples;
5. calculate removal;
6. compare against target performance;
7. repeat under representative hydraulic conditions;
8. verify integrity before and after testing;
9. document analytical detection limits;
10. distinguish experimental LRV from regulatory credit.

### Common mistake

**“If the permeate sample is below detection, the LRV is infinite.”**

Correction:

The reported removal is constrained by feed concentration, sample volume, analytical detection limit and statistics.

# 8. Algae fouling

Use the algae pages as a bridge to the Academy's dedicated algal-bloom material.

Teach:

- algae can create cell, protein, polysaccharide and AOM/TEP fouling;
- the same cell count can behave differently depending on species and bloom phase;
- coagulation can sometimes change fouling behavior;
- cleaning response depends on foulant chemistry;
- oxidants may change cells and extracellular material in ways that influence membrane fouling;
- upstream algal handling cannot be reduced to a single cell-count threshold.

Do not hard-code Pearce's historic cell-count examples as design limits.

### Interactive exercise — Cell Count Is Not Fouling Potential

Students compare two blooms with similar cell counts but different AOM/TEP/protein behavior and select monitoring/pretreatment responses.

---

# 9. Silica fouling on UF/MF

The source discusses silica as a possible UF/MF foulant, especially where high silica concentrations and changing chemistry lead to deposition.

Teach:

- dissolved silica, polymeric/colloidal silica and precipitated silicates are different forms;
- pH, temperature, ionic environment and metals such as Al can alter silica behavior;
- fouling form and solubility matter to cleaning;
- a surface deposit can be difficult to remove if transformed into low-solubility mineral/silicate phases;
- chemical cleaning must be compatible with membrane materials.

### Common mistake

**“Silica only matters in RO concentrate.”**

Correction:

Silica chemistry can affect pretreatment membranes too, depending on concentration, pH, coagulants/metals and source water.

# 10. Membrane integrity: the core lesson

This is one of the strongest sections of the uploaded batch.

Teach integrity as the ability of the complete membrane barrier to maintain separation between feed and filtrate under real operation.

Potential defect modes include:

- broken fibre;
- pinhole;
- potting/tubesheet defect;
- seal/gasket leakage;
- O-ring leakage;
- module housing leak;
- manifold/connection leak;
- damage from cleaning or pressure cycling;
- manufacturing defect;
- installation/commissioning damage.

### Common mistake

**“The membrane material has a small pore size, therefore the plant barrier is intact.”**

Correction:

The system barrier includes fibres, potting, seals, manifolds, module hardware and installation integrity.

# 11. Direct versus indirect integrity monitoring

Teach a hierarchy.

## Indirect / continuous operational indicators

Examples can include:

- filtrate turbidity;
- particle counting;
- marker/quality monitoring;
- conductivity where relevant;
- performance trends.

These are useful but may lack sensitivity for very small defects.

## Direct integrity tests

Examples from the source include:

- pressure-decay testing;
- diffusive-air-flow testing;
- vacuum-hold testing.

Teach that direct tests intentionally stress the barrier in a controlled way to detect defects beyond what normal filtrate monitoring may reveal.

### Common mistake

**“Turbidity is low, so there is no broken fibre.”**

Correction:

A small defect can be below the sensitivity of a bulk turbidity measurement while still materially affecting pathogen-barrier performance.

### Exercise — Which Integrity Test?

Students receive a module configuration, defect concern, allowable downtime and monitoring instruments and choose the appropriate testing strategy.

---

# 12. Bubble-point physics

Pearce links pressure-decay testing to the capillary/bubble-point concept.

Teach qualitatively:

- a wetted intact pore resists gas passage because of surface tension;
- the gas pressure required to displace liquid from a pore depends on pore radius, surface tension and contact angle;
- a larger defect can pass gas at a lower pressure than an intact small pore;
- therefore an integrity test can choose a pressure below the intact-membrane bubble point but high enough to reveal defects.

Do not hard-code the source's historical numerical example as a universal test pressure.

### Engineering relationship

Academy may teach the conceptual Laplace/capillary-pressure relationship with clear definitions and units, but project-grade test pressures must come from current validated manufacturer/regulatory procedures.

### Common mistake

**“Higher integrity-test pressure is always more sensitive.”**

Why it fails:

Too high a pressure may exceed safe test conditions, challenge intact pores, stress fibres or create invalid results.

---

# 13. Pressure-decay test sequence

Teach an original Academy sequence derived from the principles:

**ISOLATE → DRAIN/FILL AS REQUIRED → APPLY CLEAN GAS PRESSURE → STABILIZE → RECORD INITIAL PRESSURE → HOLD FOR DEFINED INTERVAL → RECORD DECAY → COMPARE TO ACCEPTANCE CRITERION → ISOLATE FAILED RACK/MODULE → REPAIR/RETEST → DOCUMENT**

The exact pressure, duration and acceptance criteria are current product/regulatory parameters, not Academy defaults.

### PLC exercise — Integrity Test State Machine

Students must implement:

- permissive that filtration is stopped;
- valve isolation;
- low/high pressure interlocks;
- stabilized start condition;
- timed hold;
- decay calculation;
- fail alarm;
- rack lockout;
- maintenance bypass control;
- retest requirement before return to service.

# 14. Sensitivity and rack size

A powerful lesson from the source is that test sensitivity is influenced by total intact membrane area/volume and defect magnitude.

Teach:

- the same single broken fibre can be easier to detect in a smaller tested volume than in a very large rack;
- aggregating too many modules into one integrity test can dilute the signal from a small defect;
- rack architecture and test segmentation therefore affect detectability;
- design should consider not just filtration capacity but how failures will be detected and isolated.

### Common mistake

**“One pressure-decay test across the entire plant is always most efficient.”**

Correction:

Larger test volumes can reduce defect sensitivity and make localization harder.

### Exercise — How Big Should the Integrity-Test Zone Be?

Students trade off test time, isolation valves, sensitivity, localization and CAPEX.

# 15. LRV and fibre-break reasoning

Teach LRV as:

`LRV = log10(C_feed / C_permeate)`

with strong safeguards:

- analytical detection limits matter;
- challenge concentration matters;
- dilution from intact membrane area matters;
- broken-fibre flow is not necessarily identical to intact-fibre permeate flow;
- defect hydraulics matter;
- regulatory credit may be lower than experimental performance;
- source-specific equations/examples should not be treated as universal formulas for every module family.

Pearce's historical examples comparing broken-fibre count and rack size are excellent for understanding **why barrier performance degrades nonlinearly with defects**, but not for defining 2026 allowed fibre-break limits.

### Interactive exercise — One Broken Fibre, Big Plant

Student calculates a simplified LRV impact under controlled assumptions, then identifies why the real plant requires manufacturer/regulatory validation.

# 16. Testing and repair frequency

Pearce discusses a risk-based relationship among:

- fibre-break frequency;
- number of fibres/modules;
- target LRV;
- test sensitivity;
- time between tests;
- repair strategy.

Academy should teach the logic:

**higher failure likelihood + tighter barrier requirement + larger rack = stronger case for more frequent or better-segmented integrity testing.**

Do not copy the historical weekly/monthly example as a universal requirement.

### Common mistake

**“Integrity testing should always be daily/weekly/monthly.”**

Correction:

The required frequency is a function of current regulation, product approval, failure history, risk, barrier credit and plant design.

### Exercise — How Often Should We Test?

Students receive:

- module count;
- historical defect rate;
- target barrier performance;
- regulatory requirement;
- test sensitivity;
- repair turnaround.

They must build a testing philosophy and identify what must come from current regulation.

# 17. Maintainability and repairability

Teach that design should anticipate failure.

Questions include:

- Can an individual rack/module be isolated?
- Can a broken fibre be identified?
- Can it be pinned/repaired per manufacturer procedure?
- Can the module be retested before return to service?
- Is failed equipment automatically prevented from supplying product water?
- Are integrity-test and repair records auditable?

This connects directly to Level 7 controls and Level 9 lifecycle economics.

# 18. CAPEX architecture

Pearce's historical curves should be used only to teach cost structure.

Teach major capital drivers:

- membrane area;
- module count;
- rack count;
- pressure vessels / frames;
- pumps;
- blowers;
- valves and manifolds;
- instrumentation;
- backwash system;
- CEB/CIP system;
- clean-in-place tanks/pumps;
- integrity-test hardware;
- building/footprint;
- redundancy;
- civil/electrical installation.

### Economies of scale

Teach that unit CAPEX often declines with increasing plant size because not every system component scales linearly with flow.

Do not reproduce Pearce's 2011 $/m³/d curves as current pricing.

### Common mistake

**“Double flow means double CAPEX.”**

Correction:

Scale effects, train count, minimum equipment sizes, redundancy and common infrastructure make cost scaling nonlinear.

# 19. Flux versus CAPEX

Teach the central tradeoff:

Higher design flux can reduce membrane area and module/rack CAPEX, but may increase:

- TMP;
- fouling rate;
- backwash frequency;
- CEB/CIP frequency;
- energy;
- membrane replacement;
- downtime risk.

Lower flux may increase capital but improve operating robustness.

### Exercise — Cheapest Plant or Cheapest Water?

Students compare three UF designs:

A. high flux / low area;
B. moderate flux;
C. low flux / high area.

They must compare CAPEX, OPEX, cleaning, availability and total water cost.

# 20. Source water changes economics

Pearce compares different source-water severities. Academy should preserve the principle:

The same membrane product can have very different economics on:

- clean groundwater;
- clarified surface water;
- direct raw surface water;
- seawater pretreatment;
- wastewater/reuse water.

Why:

- sustainable flux changes;
- fouling rate changes;
- cleaning requirement changes;
- pressure/energy changes;
- recovery/waste changes;
- membrane life changes;
- pretreatment requirement changes.

### Common mistake

**“UF cost is primarily a function of plant flow.”**

Correction:

Flow matters, but water quality and operating philosophy can materially change both CAPEX and OPEX.

# 21. OPEX breakdown

Teach an original operating-cost structure:

- pumping electricity;
- air scour/blower electricity;
- backwash water;
- cleaning chemicals;
- waste disposal;
- membrane replacement;
- cartridge/screen maintenance where applicable;
- instrumentation/calibration;
- labor;
- spare modules/fibres/components;
- chemical neutralization;
- downtime / lost production where economically relevant.

Pearce's historical examples show that the dominant OPEX component can shift with source water and cleaning frequency.

### Important lesson

There is no universal statement such as **“membrane replacement always dominates UF OPEX.”**

For some designs, pumping or chemicals can dominate instead.

# 22. Membrane life

Use Pearce's historical membrane-life assumptions only to teach sensitivity analysis.

Academy should ask:

- What happens if membrane life is 3 years instead of 7?
- How sensitive is total water cost to replacement price?
- Does lower flux extend useful life?
- Does aggressive cleaning accelerate ageing?
- Does better pretreatment improve life?
- How much spare inventory is required?

Do not publish one membrane-life value as a general expectation.

# 23. CIP frequency and total water cost

This is another strong teaching concept.

Pearce illustrates that total water cost can have an optimum with respect to cleaning frequency because competing costs move in opposite directions.

Teach qualitatively:

- too little cleaning → permeability loss, higher TMP/energy, lower flux/capacity and possibly irreversible fouling;
- too much cleaning → chemical cost, downtime, waste generation and membrane ageing;
- therefore the economic optimum is not necessarily the minimum or maximum cleaning frequency.

### Interactive exercise — Clean More or Clean Less?

Student is given:

- permeability decline rate;
- energy penalty;
- cleaning cost;
- downtime;
- membrane replacement sensitivity;
- water value.

They select an operating strategy and defend it.

### Common mistake

**“If CIP restores permeability, more frequent CIP is always safer.”**

Correction:

Cleaning has cost, downtime, waste and membrane-life consequences.

---

# 24. CEB versus CIP

Pearce notes that maintenance chemical cleaning and full CIP may have different efficacy/cost profiles.

Academy should teach:

- CEB can be a frequent lower-intensity maintenance intervention;
- CIP is generally a deeper restoration event;
- actual chemistry, concentration, temperature and frequency depend on membrane/foulant/current manufacturer limits;
- a higher-frequency CEB strategy may reduce CIP need in some cases, but not universally;
- the correct strategy depends on foulant type and lifecycle economics.

Do not copy historical concentrations/frequencies into current design defaults.

---

# 25. Total Water Cost (TWC)

Use the source to teach TWC as the combination of capital recovery and operating expenditure per unit water produced.

Student must understand:

- lowest CAPEX does not necessarily mean lowest TWC;
- lowest OPEX does not necessarily mean lowest TWC;
- plant utilization matters;
- membrane life matters;
- CIP/CEB strategy matters;
- source-water condition matters;
- flux matters;
- equipment redundancy affects availability and cost;
- residuals/waste disposal matters.

Connect to Total Water Economics for project-grade calculations.

### Interactive exercise — Find the Economic Sweet Spot

Student varies design flux and cleaning strategy, then compares lifecycle outcomes using a simplified Academy teaching model or, where available, validated Total Water Economics adapters.
