# Total Water Academy — Project Development, Delivery, Commercial & Finance Track v1.0

## Purpose

A water-treatment engineer should understand not only how a plant is designed, but how a technically viable project becomes an executable and financeable project. This track teaches the vocabulary, decisions, interfaces and risks that connect process engineering to project development, procurement, contracting, financing, construction, commissioning and long-term water service.

This is educational content, not legal, financial, tax or investment advice. Contract wording and project-finance structures are jurisdiction- and transaction-specific and require qualified legal, tax, insurance, financing and commercial advisers.

The track is intended primarily for Level 9, with application in the Level 10 capstone. It must remain consistent with the existing 360-guided-hour Academy architecture. The detailed Level 9 allocation should be reconciled with the core curriculum before full authoring rather than simply increasing total course hours.

### Level 10 — Commercially Defensible Capstone

The final integrated plant project should require the learner to add a concise **Project Execution & Commercial Strategy** to the technical design. The student should:

1. identify the development maturity of the project;
2. state what additional work is needed before FID;
3. select and defend an execution model;
4. identify the principal interfaces and risks;
5. explain an appropriate pricing approach;
6. identify key performance guarantees and acceptance tests;
7. identify the main contract terms that protect both owner and delivery partner;
8. determine whether project finance / an SPV is relevant;
9. if water is sold under a long-term offtake arrangement, outline the commercial structure of the WPA/Bulk Water Supply Agreement;
10. present the commercial strategy alongside the process, controls, electrical, economics and operability design.

The capstone should not require the learner to draft enforceable legal documents.

# 1. Project development stages

The Academy should teach that engineering maturity and investment maturity are related but not identical.

## 1.1 Concept / screening

Typical questions:

- What problem are we solving?
- What water source and product are contemplated?
- Is there a plausible technical solution?
- Is the project broadly affordable?
- Are site, intake/outfall, discharge, power or permitting constraints potentially fatal?
- Who is the customer/offtaker?

The output is a screening decision, not a bankable project.

## 1.2 Prefeasibility

Purpose: eliminate weak concepts and identify the few alternatives worth serious development.

Typical content:

- preliminary design basis;
- alternatives and fatal-flaw screening;
- preliminary water balance;
- preliminary technology selection;
- approximate site / utility requirements;
- preliminary CAPEX/OPEX range;
- schedule and permitting path;
- major technical/commercial risks;
- early revenue or tariff concept where relevant.

**Common misconception:** “A positive prefeasibility study means the project is ready for EPC pricing.”

Why it fails: definition, site information, permitting, interfaces and technical maturity may still be insufficient for a contractor to accept fixed-price construction risk without large contingency or exclusions.

## 1.3 Feasibility

Feasibility should test whether the preferred concept is technically, economically, legally, environmentally/socially and commercially viable, not merely whether the process calculation converges.

For a financeable infrastructure project the feasibility workstream may include:

- technical definition and output requirements;
- demand/offtake assessment;
- site and utility diligence;
- CAPEX/OPEX and lifecycle economics;
- permitting / E&S review;
- financial model;
- economic assessment where relevant;
- risk matrix;
- delivery/procurement analysis;
- market sounding;
- preliminary commercial principles.

## 1.4 Pre-FEED

Pre-FEED develops enough engineering definition to reduce uncertainty, compare configurations and prepare a defensible FEED basis. Typical water-project outputs may include process selection, preliminary PFDs, design criteria, equipment sizing philosophy, preliminary plot plan, utility loads, control philosophy, major equipment strategy and an improved cost/schedule basis.

## 1.5 FEED

FEED should establish a sufficiently mature technical and commercial basis for procurement/execution. Depending on the project and delivery method, it may include:

- final design basis for the procurement stage;
- PFDs and developed P&IDs;
- equipment specifications / data sheets;
- hydraulic and utility design bases;
- electrical load philosophy;
- control and cause/effect requirements;
- civil/site interfaces;
- constructability and modularization decisions;
- procurement strategy and long-lead equipment identification;
- execution schedule;
- performance guarantees and test philosophy;
- battery limits and owner/contractor interfaces;
- updated estimate and risk register.

**Important:** “FEED” is used differently across companies and sectors. Academy should teach the purpose and maturity concept rather than pretend one universal percentage of engineering defines FEED.

## 1.6 Final Investment Decision (FID)

FID is an investment/governance decision, not simply “the design is finished.” The sponsor/owner decides whether to commit capital and proceed based on the maturity of the technical, commercial, permitting, financing and risk package.

Learners should check, at a conceptual level:

- technical basis sufficiently mature;
- cost/schedule understood within accepted uncertainty;
- major permits/site rights on a credible path;
- revenue/offtake case acceptable;
- key contracts sufficiently developed;
- financing plan and lender requirements understood;
- material risks allocated/mitigated;
- owner approvals obtained.

FID should be distinguished from **financial close**. Financial close is the point at which financing agreements and their conditions precedent are satisfied so funds can be drawn. The two events may be close in time but are not synonymous.

# 2. Execution model versus pricing model

A critical Academy lesson is that **scope-delivery structure and compensation structure are different questions**.

“EPC” describes a responsibility bundle: engineering, procurement and construction. It does **not automatically mean lump sum**. An EPC scope can be commercially structured in different ways depending on the contract.

Likewise, “cost plus,” “reimbursable,” “GMP,” “unit rate” and “lump sum” are primarily compensation/risk mechanisms, not complete delivery models by themselves.

## 2.1 EP

Engineering + Procurement normally places design and procurement responsibility with the EP contractor while construction is contracted separately or performed by the owner/another construction manager.

Teach:

- vendor package definition;
- procurement management;
- construction interface risk;
- constructability feedback needs;
- responsibility for field changes;
- schedule interfaces between supplier and constructor.

## 2.2 EP + Supply

Common in process-water packages where a technology company or integrator provides engineering plus specified equipment/material supply while civil construction, installation or balance-of-plant remains elsewhere.

Core lesson: **battery limits and interface definition become critical**.

Typical mistakes:

- assuming “supply” includes installation;
- unclear responsibility for interconnecting piping/cabling;
- missing commissioning consumables or temporary utilities;
- conflicting controls interfaces;
- unclear freight/tax/incoterm/title/risk-of-loss responsibility;
- no single party responsible for overall plant performance.

## 2.3 EPC

The EPC contractor accepts responsibility for the engineering, procurement and construction within the defined scope and contract terms.

The student must ask:

- What exactly is inside the EPC battery limits?
- What information remains Owner-provided?
- Is the price lump sum, GMP, target cost, reimbursable or hybrid?
- What risks are included/excluded?
- What performance is guaranteed?
- What relief events allow time/cost adjustment?

## 2.4 LSTK / EPC Turnkey

Lump Sum Turnkey EPC seeks high certainty of completion price, date and performance by transferring a broad package of responsibility to one contractor. In return, contractors price the risks they are asked to accept.

Academy must explicitly teach:

> Turnkey does not mean “the owner has no risk.”

Owner obligations, defined relief events, change in scope, force majeure, permits, access, feed/utility conditions, interface obligations and other retained risks still matter.

## 2.5 Hybrid EPC — fixed E/P + cost-plus Construction

The Academy should include the execution model the user identified: Engineering and Procurement may be negotiated on a lump-sum/fixed basis while Construction is reimbursed on a cost-plus or other open-book basis.

This can be useful where:

- engineering/procurement scope can be defined earlier than field construction;
- site quantities or execution conditions remain uncertain;
- schedule requires early procurement before construction scope is fully mature;
- the owner wants transparency and retains part of construction cost risk.

Students should compare this against an LSTK EPC and ask who bears:

- productivity risk;
- quantity growth;
- subcontractor escalation;
- field rework;
- schedule acceleration;
- site conditions;
- contractor fee/overhead;
- contingency usage.

## 2.6 Design-Build and Progressive Design-Build

Traditional Design-Build provides a single point of responsibility for design and construction. Progressive Design-Build deliberately develops the technical definition and commercial commitment progressively after selection.

For PDB, teach the two-phase logic:

**Selection → Phase 1 validation/design/preconstruction → target budget/open-book estimating → negotiated Phase 2 commercial proposal/GMP or other agreed price → Phase 2 final design/construction → commissioning/handover.**

The owner generally retains an **off-ramp** if Phase 2 commercial terms cannot be agreed, subject to the actual contract.

Do not teach PDB as “EPC with a late price.” The collaboration, procurement basis, transparency, owner participation, progressive definition, risk-development process and off-ramp are central to the delivery method.

## 2.7 DBO / DBOM / DBOOM and privately financed variants

Lifecycle delivery adds operating responsibility and can change design incentives because the same private party may bear long-term reliability, energy, chemical, maintenance and replacement consequences.

The Academy should distinguish:

- **DBO** — design, build, operate;
- **DBOM** — design, build, operate, maintain;
- **DBOOM** — design, build, own, operate, maintain, where used by the transaction;
- **BOO** — build, own, operate;
- **BOT / BOOT** — build/own (where applicable), operate and transfer at contract end;
- **DBFOM / DBFOMT** — includes private financing and possibly transfer at expiry.

Acronym usage varies by jurisdiction and transaction. Students must read the actual contract structure rather than infer risk allocation from the acronym alone.

# 3. Progressive Design-Build learning block

The provided FHWA/DBIA material strongly supports a rigorous PDB lesson.

## 3.1 Core concepts

- selection can occur before substantial design is complete;
- owner and design-builder develop design and commercial definition together;
- cost feedback is introduced while alternatives are still being evaluated;
- open-book estimating supports transparency;
- price, schedule and performance commitments can be deferred until definition is sufficient;
- Phase 1 is typically preconstruction/design-development work;
- Phase 2 proceeds after agreement on the commercial proposal;
- an off-ramp can protect the owner if Phase 2 terms are not acceptable.

## 3.2 GMP is not the same as lump sum

A **Guaranteed Maximum Price** establishes a ceiling subject to the actual contract's exclusions, allowances, contingencies and permitted changes. Open-book cost-plus/GMP mechanisms can coexist with lump-sum subscopes or later conversion to lump sum.

Academy should force learners to identify:

- Cost of the Work;
- contractor/design-builder fee;
- general conditions;
- allowances;
- owner contingency versus contractor/design-builder contingency where applicable;
- NTE scopes;
- GMP assumptions and clarifications;
- permitted change events;
- savings treatment;
- audit/open-book rights.

## 3.3 PDB risk challenge

Learners receive a water project with uncertain raw-water intake works, a well-defined membrane package and a long-lead electrical substation. They must decide:

- what to validate during Phase 1;
- which trade partners should be brought in early;
- what can be procured early;
- what should remain open-book;
- what design maturity is needed before fixing price;
- what belongs in allowance or contingency;
- whether the owner should accept the Phase 2 proposal or use the off-ramp.

# 4. Project finance and bankability

## 4.1 Corporate finance versus project finance

In corporate finance, repayment depends broadly on the sponsoring company's balance sheet and business. In project finance, financing is structured primarily around the project's contractual cash flows and risk allocation, commonly through an SPV.

At Academy depth, students should understand the parties:

- sponsors/equity investors;
- SPV/project company;
- lenders;
- offtaker / water purchaser;
- EPC contractor;
- O&M contractor;
- technology/equipment suppliers;
- government/contracting authority where relevant;
- insurers/sureties;
- advisers and independent engineer/lender technical adviser.

## 4.2 What “bankable” means

A project is not bankable merely because the NPV is positive.

Lenders evaluate whether the complete risk and cash-flow structure has credible mechanisms for:

- completion;
- cost overruns;
- delay;
- performance shortfall;
- feed/utility availability;
- water quality compliance;
- O&M reliability;
- offtaker payment;
- force majeure;
- political/change-in-law risk where relevant;
- insurance;
- termination compensation;
- lender security and step-in rights.

## 4.3 Basic financial literacy

Teach, without turning Academy into an investment-banking qualification:

- equity versus debt;
- construction and operating cash flow;
- debt service;
- debt-service reserve concepts;
- DSCR as cash available for debt service divided by debt service;
- tariff/indexation;
- sensitivity cases;
- contingency and reserve accounts;
- base case versus downside case;
- financial close.

Financial calculations that later belong in Total Water Economics should come through the authoritative economics owner rather than a parallel Academy finance model.

# 5. Water Purchase Agreement / Bulk Water Supply Agreement

For privately financed or long-term service water projects, the offtake agreement can be as important to bankability as the EPC contract.

## 5.1 Commercial purpose

The WPA/Bulk Water Supply Agreement defines how the purchaser pays for compliant water made available and/or delivered over the contract term.

Typical concepts to teach:

- contracted capacity;
- minimum offtake / take-or-pay where used;
- availability/capacity payments;
- variable payment for actual water delivered;
- tariff and indexation;
- metering and measurement point;
- water-quality specification;
- availability and reliability requirements;
- testing and acceptance;
- planned/unplanned outage rules;
- deductions / performance LDs;
- energy/feed-water responsibilities where applicable;
- change in law;
- force majeure;
- payment security;
- credit support;
- lender step-in/direct agreement concepts;
- default and cure;
- termination payment;
- handback/transfer if the concession model requires it.

## 5.2 Common misconception

**Mistake:** “Take-or-pay means the water company is paid for any quantity it produces regardless of performance.”

Correction: the actual payment mechanism, availability requirements, quality requirements, deductions, deemed availability, dispatch/offtake obligations and relief events are contract-specific. Students must read the payment and performance mechanisms together.

## 5.3 WPA interactive mission

Give the learner a 100,000 m³/d desalination plant with a project-financed SPV. The learner chooses between:

- pure volumetric payment;
- fixed capacity + variable water charge;
- minimum offtake/take-or-pay structure.

Then introduce:

- low demand;
- high power cost;
- an unavailable train;
- off-spec boron;
- delayed payment by the offtaker;
- a change in electricity tariff methodology.

The learner must explain which party bears each risk and which clause/payment mechanism should address it.

# 7. Guided Engineering Solution Mode for commercial problems

Hard commercial exercises should use the Academy's Guided Engineering Solution Mode.

## Example: Choose the execution model

### Step 1 — Define the project

A municipality wants a 60 MGD advanced reuse plant. Process concept is selected but geotechnical data, final site utilities and building quantities are immature. Long-lead membranes and electrical equipment need early procurement.

### Step 2 — Identify the decision

Choose between:

- LSTK EPC;
- hybrid EPC with reimbursable construction;
- PDB;
- DBO/DBOM;
- privately financed DBOOM/BOOT-type structure if policy and revenue model support it.

### Step 3 — Ask what is known and unknown

Before choosing a contract, identify:

- design maturity;
- owner resources;
- cost certainty need;
- schedule urgency;
- interface complexity;
- market appetite;
- long-term O&M objectives;
- financing strategy;
- revenue/offtake structure.

### Common mistake card

**Mistake / misconception:** “LSTK is always safest for the owner because all risk is transferred.”

**Why it is tempting:** a fixed price and completion date look like complete certainty.

**Why it fails:** risk can only be transferred effectively when it is definable, contractually allocated and commercially accepted. Unclear risks may return through qualifications, contingency, claims, reduced bidder competition or high pricing.

**How to detect it:** review exclusions, owner obligations, relief events, interface assumptions and bidder qualifications rather than only the headline price.

**How to correct it:** allocate each risk to the party best able to manage it and choose the execution/pricing model consistent with project maturity.

# 8. Misconception library

The Academy should deliberately call out at least the following:

1. **“EPC means lump sum.”** No. EPC describes scope/responsibility; the price mechanism must be read separately.
2. **“Turnkey means zero owner risk.”** No. Owner obligations and retained/relief risks remain.
3. **“GMP means lump sum.”** No. A GMP may sit over cost-plus/open-book compensation and agreed contingencies/allowances.
4. **“Cost plus means no cost control.”** Not inherently. NTEs, GMPs, audit rights, fee structures, target budgets, approvals and shared savings may be used.
5. **“FEED means the design is complete.”** No. FEED establishes an execution/procurement basis; detailed design remains.
6. **“FID and financial close are the same.”** No. FID is an investment approval decision; financial close establishes executable financing.
7. **“A technically feasible project is bankable.”** Not necessarily. Revenue, contractual risk allocation, counterparties and financing also matter.
8. **“A WPA is just a water price.”** No. It also defines quantity, quality, availability, payment, risk, default and relief mechanisms.
9. **“The lowest EPC price is the lowest project cost.”** Not necessarily. Scope gaps, exclusions, lifecycle energy/O&M and claims exposure matter.
10. **“Risk transfer is free.”** No. Contractors, lenders and insurers price risk.
11. **“PDB is simply EPC before the price is known.”** No. Progressive collaboration, open-book development, staged commitment and the off-ramp are defining characteristics.
12. **“An engineer does not need to read the contract.”** Incorrect. Process guarantees, battery limits, feed conditions, tests and acceptance criteria are engineering obligations embedded in contracts.

# 9. Interactive Academy missions

## Mission A — Delivery Model Match

Give five projects with different maturity, owner capability, financing and risk profiles. Learners select EP, EP+Supply, EPC, LSTK EPC, hybrid reimbursable EPC, PDB, DBOM or DBOOM/PPP structure and defend the choice.

## Mission B — Scope Gap Detective

Show an EP+Supply scope with missing installation/control/battery-limit responsibilities. Student identifies interfaces likely to become change orders.

## Mission C — PDB Phase 1 to Phase 2

Student develops a validation plan, target budget, early procurement strategy and risk register, then decides whether enough definition exists to establish the Phase 2 commercial proposal/GMP.

## Mission D — GMP Builder

Interactive cost stack:

Direct work + subcontracts + general conditions + allowances + contingencies + design-builder fee → GMP.

Students must decide which movement is a normal Cost of Work issue versus a Change Order event under the teaching scenario.

## Mission E — FID Gate

Student receives technical, permitting, commercial and financing status cards and decides whether to recommend:

- continue development;
- conditional FID;
- FID;
- stop/restructure.

## Mission F — Bankability Room

The learner sits in a simulated lender technical/commercial review and must explain completion risk, performance risk, EPC wrap, O&M, offtake and payment security.

## Mission G — Build the WPA

Assemble an original Academy payment mechanism using capacity and variable charges, water-quality/availability conditions and take-or-pay concepts. Then stress-test it against demand, power-cost and plant-performance scenarios.

## Mission H — Contract Red-Flag Review

Students review an original Academy term sheet and identify:

- missing battery limit;
- impossible guarantee;
- uncapped LD exposure;
- conflicting acceptance tests;
- undefined feed-water condition;
- unclear escalation/indexation;
- missing change-order mechanism;
- unallocated permit;
- ambiguous milestone.

## Mission I — Capstone Commercial Wrap

For the Level 10 treatment plant, submit:

- project development stage;
- selected execution model;
- commercial rationale;
- risk allocation matrix;
- high-level contract terms;
- financing/offtake concept;
- FID readiness statement.

# 10. Source and copyright policy

## Sources supplied for PDB / DB contracting content

The Academy may use the engineering/project-delivery concepts, with provenance, from the user's supplied materials including:

- Design-Build Institute of America, **DBIA Document No. 530 — Standard Form of Agreement Between Owner and Design-Builder — Cost Plus Fee with an Option for a Guaranteed Maximum Price**, Third Edition, 2022.
- U.S. Department of Transportation / Federal Highway Administration Resource Center, **Progressive Design-Build**, 2020.
- M. Alazzam, A. Celoza and K. R. Molenaar, **Progressive Design-Build Opportunities and Challenges: A Literature Review**, Joint CSCE Construction Specialty & CRC Conference 2025.
- Design-Build Institute of America, **Design-Build and Progressive Design-Build Key Characteristics — A Deeper Dive**, 2018.
- DBIA Document No. 625, **Payment Bond for Design-Build Projects**, First Edition, 2015, for awareness of bond concepts only.

### Copyright safeguard

DBIA contract documents are copyrighted/licensed materials and the supplied DBIA license expressly restricts reproduction for educational purposes. Therefore Total Water Academy must **not reproduce DBIA contract forms, clauses, figures or tables**. Academy must create original diagrams, original scenarios and paraphrased educational explanations, and may identify the source for further reading where permitted.

## Additional authoritative public reference families for topics not covered by the supplied PDB documents

The supplied files primarily support PDB, GMP/open-book compensation and design-build contract concepts. They do **not by themselves** fully source EPC/LSTK, project finance, FID or Water Purchase Agreement teaching. Those sections should rely on separate authoritative references such as:

- FIDIC EPC/Turnkey (Silver Book) public descriptions/guidance for EPC/turnkey responsibility and risk concepts;
- World Bank PPP Resource Center / PPP Reference Guide for project preparation, feasibility, risk allocation, financial close, BOT/BOOT/DBO structures and water-sector agreements;
- World Bank/IFC water and desalination PPP case material for long-term bulk-water offtake and project-finance examples;
- current transaction-specific manufacturer, legal and financial sources where a lesson claims a current market practice.

The Academy must distinguish general industry education from legal advice and must not imply that a sample clause or allocation is universally appropriate.

# 11. Current-market case-study direction

Potential original Academy cases can be based on public patterns without copying proprietary contracts:

- municipally funded reuse plant choosing PDB versus LSTK EPC;
- industrial demineralization plant delivered EP + Supply with owner-managed construction;
- mining desalination system using EPC plus long-term O&M;
- project-financed seawater desalination plant with an SPV and bulk Water Purchase Agreement;
- ZLD facility where uncertain civil/site scope favors a hybrid EPC commercial model;
- expansion around an operating plant where interface/operations risk dominates delivery choice.

Case studies should be updated periodically because procurement law, financing markets, interest rates, insurance appetite and delivery-model practice change over time.

# 12. Success criterion

A successful Academy graduate should be able to answer:

> **What stage is this project in, what information is still missing, who should own each risk, what execution model fits the maturity and objectives, how will the contractor be paid, what must the contract guarantee, and—if privately financed—what makes the revenue and financing structure bankable?**

That is the commercial/project-development literacy expected of a modern water engineer without pretending the engineer is the project's lawyer or investment banker.
